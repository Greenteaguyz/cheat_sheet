Core definition

Verb patterns describe what form follows a verb: a gerund (-ing), an infinitive (to + verb), or a bare infinitive. Each verb takes its own pattern, so you need to learn the groups.

Trap: The -ing form has different jobs: a gerund is a noun ("Studying is tiring"); a present participle is a verb or adjective ("I am studying", "an interesting movie").

  • I enjoy having a bath in the evening. (gerund)
  • I want to leave early. (infinitive)
  • The movie is interesting. (participle as adjective)

1. Verbs followed by a gerund

Feelings: enjoy, dislike, fancy, mind, appreciate, tolerate, miss, can't stand, can't help.
Doing and avoiding: avoid, finish, complete, keep, delay, postpone, practice, risk, involve, deserve.
Talking and thinking: discuss, suggest, recommend, admit, deny, mention, report, recall, consider, imagine, anticipate, understand.

Trap: Suggest and recommend take a gerund (not "suggest me to go"). Learn them as a list.

  • She avoided telling him about her plans.
  • Do you mind giving me a hand?
  • She admitted stealing the money.

2. Verbs followed by an infinitive

Plans and wishes: decide, hope, plan, want, intend, expect, arrange, choose, prepare, would like/love/hate.
Agreeing and offering: promise, agree, offer, refuse, demand, ask.
Ability and appearance: manage, afford, learn, pretend, claim, seem, appear, tend.

Trap: Expect takes an infinitive, but anticipate takes a gerund: "I expect to arrive" vs "I anticipate arriving".

  • They plan to start college in the autumn.
  • I promise to help you tomorrow.
  • Unfortunately, we can't afford to buy a new car.

3. Verb + object + infinitive, and make / let / have / get

Object + infinitive and bare infinitive

verb + object + (to) infinitive

Object + infinitive: want, ask, expect, would prefer.
Bare infinitive: make and let (help takes either).

Trap: Don't put "to" after make: "She makes me carry" (not "to carry"). After recommend and suggest, don't use object + infinitive: say "I recommend taking" or "I recommend that you take".

Make, let, have, get

Make/let/have + object + bare infinitive.
Get + object + to-infinitive.
Have/get + object + past participle (for services).

Trap: Get needs to; make doesn't.

  • I would prefer you to come early.
  • She makes me carry the bags.
  • She helped me (to) carry my suitcases.
  • She makes me carry the bags.
  • I got him to help.
  • We had the car repaired.

4. Preposition + gerund

After a preposition

After a preposition, always use the -ing form, even when the preposition is "to".

Trap: In look forward to, accustomed to, and devoted to, "to" is a preposition. Use -ing, not the base verb.

Adjective + preposition

about: excited, worried
for: known, famous, sorry
of: proud, scared, sick
on: keen · to: addicted · in: interested

Trap: The adjective decides the preposition. Learn each pair together.

Verb or noun + preposition

Verbs: carry on, keep on, insist on, succeed in, cope with, worry about, blame (someone) for.
Nouns: habit of, talent for, experience in, reputation for, disadvantage of.

Trap: The preposition belongs to the verb or noun, not the gerund. Don't drop it.

  • She is looking forward to visiting her aunt.
  • I'm tired of repeating the same things.
  • He apologized for being late.
  • My wife is keen on singing pop songs.
  • The actor is famous for being crazy once in a while.
  • The hikers are worried about not having enough water.
  • She succeeds in keeping the children busy.
  • He insisted on taking the dog for a walk.
  • She has a talent for learning languages.

5. Verbs that take both (meaning changes)

Some verbs take either form, but the meaning differs.

Trap: Watch the time order: stop doing = quit; stop to do = pause in order to do something.

  • I remember locking the door. (I did it) / I remembered to lock the door. (I did not forget)
  • He stopped smoking. (quit) / He stopped to smoke. (paused in order to smoke)
  • I tried adding salt. (experiment) / I tried to add salt. (attempt)

6. Verbs that take both forms with little change

Begin, start, continue, like, love, hate, prefer take either -ing or to + verb with almost the same meaning. After would like/love/hate/prefer, use the infinitive.

Trap: "I'd like swimming" is wrong. "I like swimming" is a general liking; "I'd like to swim" is a wish.

  • She began to cry / crying.
  • I like swimming. / I would like to swim today.
  • He prefers to walk / walking.

7. Used to / be used to / get used to

Used to + base verb = a past habit. Be/get used to + -ing or noun = accustomed to.

Trap: "I'm used to get up early" is wrong. "To" here is a preposition, so use -ing.

  • I used to live in Spain.
  • I am used to getting up early.
  • She got used to working nights.

8. Fixed -ing expressions

Some expressions are always followed by -ing: spend/waste time, go + activity, it's worth, it's no use, have trouble/difficulty, be busy, can't help.

Trap: Don't add "to" or "in" after them in these frames.

  • He spends hours studying.
  • They went shopping.
  • It's no use complaining. / It's worth visiting.

9. Too / enough + infinitive

Too + adjective + to + verb (negative result). Adjective + enough + to + verb (positive result).

Trap: Enough comes after the adjective but before a noun: "enough money".

  • It is too cold to swim.
  • She is old enough to vote.
  • We don't have enough time to finish.

10. Passive and perfect forms

Gerund: being + p.p. (passive), having + p.p. (perfect).
Infinitive: to be + p.p. (passive), to have + p.p. (perfect).

Trap: Use the passive when the subject receives the action; use the perfect when the action happened earlier.

  • She hates being interrupted.
  • He admitted having lied.
  • The report is to be finished today. / He seems to have left.

Quick checklist

  • Verb is on the gerund list? Use -ing.
  • Verb is on the infinitive list? Use to + verb.
  • Preposition before the verb? Always -ing.
  • make/let? Bare infinitive. suggest/recommend? Gerund.
  • remember, stop, try? Check which meaning you intend.

Core definition

A conditional sentence has an if clause (the condition) and a main clause (the result). The clause order does not change the meaning.

Use a comma only when the if clause comes first.

Trap: The tense in the if clause shows how real or likely the situation is, not when it happens.

  • If you study hard, you will pass your exams.
  • You will pass your exams if you study hard.

1. Zero conditional (real, general truths)

If + present simple, present simple

Use it for facts and repeated results: something always happens when a condition is met.

Trap: Don't use will in either clause. "If" can usually be replaced by "when".

  • If you add two and two, you get four.
  • If he takes vitamins every day, he doesn't get sick.
  • He doesn't get sick if he takes vitamins every day.

2. First conditional (likely future)

If + present simple, will + infinitive

Use it for a possible or likely outcome in the present or future.

Trap: Never put will in the if clause. "As soon as" and "when" also use the present: "I'll have a shower as soon as I get home."

  • If it rains later, we 'll stay at home.
  • If she studies for the test, she will get a good grade.
  • We 'll be late if we don't hurry up.

3. Second conditional (unreal or unlikely present/future)

If + past simple, would + infinitive

Use it for impossible or unlikely situations and their imagined results.

Trap: The past tense here is not about past time. Use were for all persons in formal English ("If I were you…"). Don't put would in the if clause.

  • If I won the lottery, I would sail round the world.
  • If I were you, I would get a new job.
  • If I had wings, I would fly to Antarctica.

4. Third conditional (unreal past)

If + past perfect, would have + past participle

Use it for past regrets or different outcomes: things that did not happen and their imagined results.

Trap: Don't use would have in the if clause. It is about the past, so "had" is needed.

  • If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam.
  • If you hadn't been late, we would not have missed the bus.
  • If we had arrived earlier, we would have seen John.

5. Mixed conditionals

Not in the course handouts (added).

Past cause, present result / Present state, past result

Mix two time frames.

Past condition, present result: if + past perfect, would + infinitive.
Present condition, past result: if + past simple, would have + past participle.

Trap: Look at the time of each clause separately. Each clause follows its own time, not the usual pattern.

  • If I had listened to my mother, I would be happier now.
  • If I were a better driver, I wouldn't have had that accident.

6. What 'inversion' means

Subject + auxiliary → Auxiliary + subject

To invert is to put the auxiliary or modal before the subject. If there is no auxiliary, use do/does/did.

Inversion adds emphasis or formality.

Trap: Don't invert a plain verb: "Won I" is wrong. Use should, were or had in conditionals.

  • She will visit her aunt. → Will she visit her aunt?
  • She visited her aunt. → Did she visit her aunt?
  • She was tired. → Was she tired?

7. Inversion: should, were, had

First (should)

If + subject + verb → Should + subject + base verb

Drop if, put should before the subject, and use the base verb. It sounds formal and makes the situation seem a little less likely. It is often used for polite requests or offers.

Trap: Use should only for the first conditional. Don't add "to" after it.

Second (were)

If + past → Were + subject + to + infinitive

Drop if, start with were, and use to + infinitive. With the verb be, just move were before the subject.

Trap: You can't invert the simple past directly. Not "Won I the lottery…", but "Were I to win…". With "be", "to" is not needed.

Third (had)

If + had + p.p. → Had + subject + p.p.

Drop if and move had before the subject. Everything else stays the same.

Trap: Keep the past participle. Don't add have or a second "had".

  • Should a customer wish to cancel, they should notify us within seven days.
  • Should you need any assistance, please contact me.
  • Were the Sun to disappear, Earth would no longer support life.
  • Were California a country, it would be one of the world's largest economies.
  • Were I to get married, I would work hard.
  • Had we lost that match, it would've been a disaster.
  • We would have fixed that problem by now had we known earlier.
  • Had I realized the consequences, I would never have taken that decision.

8. Negative inversion

Should/Were/Had + subject + not …

Put not after the subject, never contract it before the subject.

Trap: Don't write "Hadn't I been busy…" in formal writing.

  • Should you not consent, you may inform our company at any time.
  • Were I not good at math, I 'd find this homework challenging.
  • Had I not been busy, I would have attended the meeting.

9. Alternatives to 'if'

Not in the course handouts (added). Compare the adverb clause condition card.

Other words can introduce a condition: unless (= if not), provided/providing (that), as long as, on condition that, supposing, only if. Otherwise (= if not) links two sentences.

Trap: Don't use unless with a negative verb. In case means "because it may happen", not "if".

  • You will pass provided that you study.
  • Unless you hurry, you will miss the bus.
  • Hurry up; otherwise, we will miss the bus.

10. If + am/is/are to, and were to

Only the "I am to get married" and "Sun were to disappear" examples are from the slides. The explanation and the other example are added.

If + am/is/are to + verb, will/must/should + verb

If + be to expresses a necessary or intended future (formal). If + were to expresses an unlikely or imagined future.

Trap: This is not the same as the plain present: "If I am to get married" means "if I intend to" or "if it is planned".

  • If I am to get married, I will work hard.
  • If we are to finish on time, we must start now.
  • If the Sun were to disappear, Earth would no longer support life.

Core definition

Conjunctive adverbs (however, therefore, moreover) show how one idea relates to the previous one. They do not join clauses on their own.

Conjunctions do join clauses:
Coordinating: and, but, so
Subordinating: although, because, while
Correlative: not only…but also

Trap: Ask: does this word join two clauses by itself? If yes, it is a conjunction. If it needs a period or semicolon, it is a conjunctive adverb.

  • Lisa and Alan were on time. However, John arrived late.
  • Although John arrived late, the meeting started on time.

1. Punctuating and placing conjunctive adverbs

Punctuation (avoid the comma splice)

Sentence. Adverb, sentence. / Sentence; adverb, sentence.

A conjunctive adverb needs a period or semicolon before it. It is followed by a comma when it starts the sentence.

Trap: Joining two sentences with only a comma before the adverb is a comma splice (wrong): "There was a scandal, therefore, the president resigned."

Placement

They can open the sentence (most common), sit between subject and verb, or close the sentence. Set them off with commas.

Trap: Even in the middle or at the end, they do not join clauses. You still need a period or semicolon before the sentence.

  • There was a scandal. Therefore, the company's president resigned.
  • There was a scandal; therefore, the company's president resigned.
  • The employees were tired. As a result, an accident occurred.
  • For example, people like walking with their dogs.
  • People, for example, like walking with their dogs.
  • Many customers were upset, nonetheless.

2. Functions of conjunctive adverbs

Choose the adverb by the relationship you want to show.

Trap: Don't use however to add information or moreover to show contrast. The word must match the meaning.

"On the contrary" vs "on the other hand"

On the contrary rejects the previous statement entirely. On the other hand adds a different side of the same issue.

Trap: Both show contrast, but you can't swap them.

  • Addition: Bananas are healthy. In addition, they are easy to eat. (also: moreover, furthermore, besides)
  • Contrast: The owner was rich. However, most employees were poor. (also: nevertheless, nonetheless, in contrast, on the other hand)
  • Example: Many sports are played in winter. For example, people enjoy cross-country skiing. (also: for instance)
  • Emphasis or detail: There are many areas Joe wants to visit in Europe. In particular, he really wants to see France and Italy. (also: of course, undoubtedly, in fact, indeed, particularly, specifically)
  • Result: University is costly. Therefore, many people cannot afford it. (also: thus, hence, consequently, as a result)
  • Order and time: They sat down. Next, they were given a form. (also: first, then, meanwhile, finally)
  • Conclusion: In conclusion, smoking should be banned. (also: in summary, to sum up, overall)
  • Black bears don't usually attack humans. On the contrary, they normally run away.
  • The book seems to be about success. On the contrary, it teaches lessons about failure.
  • The job pays well. On the other hand, the hours are long.

3. Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS)

For, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. They join two independent clauses with a comma before the conjunction.

Trap: Don't join two clauses with a comma alone. "So" and "for" always need a comma before them.

  • He was tired of commuting, so he was excited about the job offer.
  • Practice diving, for staying under too long can cause illness.
  • The residents were warned, yet several people were injured.

4. Subordinating conjunctions (adverb clauses)

Full list by type: Adverb clauses sheet.

They begin a dependent clause that shows time, contrast, reason, purpose, condition, or concession. The clause cannot stand alone.

Trap: Put a comma after the clause when it comes first, but usually not when it comes last.

  • Contrast: Western Scotland is wild, whereas eastern Scotland is lush farmland. (also: while, although, even though, much as)
  • Time: Traffic gets lighter once the schools close. (also: as soon as, until, by the time, since)
  • Condition: The boys can go fishing as long as they come back before dark.
  • Purpose or precaution: Take a blanket in case it gets cooler. / In order to relieve the burden, they will build a second airport.

5. Clause vs. phrase: although / despite, because / due to

Although + S + V / Despite + noun or -ing

Followed by a clause (S + V): although, even though, because.
Followed by a noun phrase or gerund: despite, in spite of, due to, owing to, thanks to, because of.

Trap: Don't use despite or due to before a full clause. "Despite it was windy" is wrong; "Despite the wind" is right.

  • Even though it was extremely windy, we played volleyball.
  • Despite the wind, we played volleyball.
  • Due to the renovation of their stadium, the team will use another ground.

6. Correlative conjunctions

both…and / either…or / neither…nor / whether…or / not only…but also

They come in pairs and must link parallel structures (same part of speech or form).

Trap: Keep both parts parallel. Don't mix them, such as "both X or Y". The verb agrees with the nearer subject (see the subject-verb agreement sheet).

  • Students must study either French or Spanish.
  • The plan is neither costly nor painful.
  • The society helps students not only settle in but also organises trips.

7. Inversion with negative correlatives

Hardly + had + S + p.p. + when / No sooner + had + S + p.p. + than

After not only, hardly, scarcely, no sooner at the start of a sentence, the auxiliary comes before the subject. Pair them with but also / when / than.

Trap: Hardly/scarcely go with when, and no sooner goes with than. Don't swap them.

  • Hardly had she placed the cakes on the table when the children asked for more.
  • No sooner had we arrived than it began to rain.
  • Not only does the society help students settle in, but it also organises trips.

8. "No matter" and the -ever words

No matter how + adj/adv + S + V / However + adj + S + V

No matter how/what/who and however, whatever, whoever, whichever, whenever, wherever mean "it does not matter". Use however or no matter how with an adjective or adverb.

Trap: However alone (with no adjective) is a conjunctive adverb for contrast. Whichever goes with a noun ("whichever channel").

  • No matter how hard the authorities try, crime stays high.
  • Whichever channel you watch, the news is the same.
  • No matter how many windows I open, there is no air movement.

9. Purpose, otherwise, instead, besides, whether…or not

So that / in order that introduce purpose (usually with can/could/will/would).
Otherwise (= if not), instead (in place of that), and besides (in addition) are conjunctive adverbs.
Whether…or not introduces alternatives.

Trap: Besides means "also"; beside means "next to". So that (purpose) is different from so…that (result).

  • He studies so that he can improve his English.
  • He didn't call. Instead, he sent an email.
  • Go now; otherwise, you will be late.

Quick checklist

  • Two full sentences? Use a period or semicolon plus a conjunctive adverb, or a conjunction.
  • Clause after the connector? Use although, because, while. Noun or -ing? Use despite, due to, because of.
  • Pairs like both…and or hardly…when? Keep both parts parallel and use the correct partner.
  • Is the relationship addition, contrast, example, result, time, or conclusion? Choose the adverb that matches.

Core definition

Subordinating conjunction + subject + verb

An adverb clause is a dependent clause that acts as an adverb. It tells when, where, why, how or under what condition something happens, and modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb.

Trap: It cannot stand alone. Related connectors: Conjunctions sheet.

  • I stayed home because it was raining. (why)
  • If you study, you will pass the test. (condition)

1. Position and punctuation

The clause can come before or after the independent clause.

Before: put a comma after it. After: no comma. Exception: always use a comma with while and whereas. Many writers also always use one with although, though, even though.

Trap: Avoid double connectors: not because…so, not although…but.

  • Because he was sick, he stayed home.
  • He finished his work even though he was tired.

2. Time and place

Time: when (a specific time), whenever (any time), while (at the same time), as soon as, after (later), before (earlier), since (from that time), as (at the same time), until (up to the time).
Place: where, wherever, everywhere, anywhere.

Trap: Don't use a future tense in a time clause: "I will call you when I arrive", not "when I will arrive".

  • I will call you when I get home.
  • Call me before you leave.
  • I'll get coffee while you two talk.
  • Most people shop where they get the lowest prices.
  • Anywhere you go, you hear people talking on their cell phones.

3. Frequency and manner

Frequency (how often?): as + adverb + as; it follows the independent clause.
Manner (how?): as, as if, as though. Most manner clauses follow the independent clause.

Trap: Use as if / as though + past tense for unreal situations: "as if he were the manager", not "like he is".

  • I do not visit my parents as often as they would like me to.
  • He speaks as though he were the boss.
  • Do it as I told you.

4. Reason, result, and purpose

Reason: because, since, as (before or after the main clause).
Result: so + adjective/adverb + that; such a(n) + noun + that. A result clause follows the independent clause.

  • Europeans are better environmentalists because they are more used to conserving energy.
  • Joanna's cookie business is so successful that she hired three new employees.
  • Joanna's cookies are such a success that she is considering franchising.
  • He studies every day so that he can improve his English. (added)

5. Contrast and concession

Direct opposition: whereas, while.
Concession (unexpected result): although, even though, though. It means "this is true, but the main clause is more important".

Trap: Don't pair although with but. One connector is enough.

  • San Francisco is cool during the summer, whereas Los Angeles is generally hot.
  • Although I had studied all night, I failed the test.
  • Though the citizens had despised the old regime, they disliked the new government even more.

6. Condition: if and unless

Full coverage, including inversion: Conditionals sheet.

If = the condition must happen. Unless = the condition must not happen.

PatternIf clauseMain clause
Present, truepresentpresent
Future, truepresentfuture (will)
Present/future, untruesimple pastwould + base
Past, untruepast perfectwould have + p.p.

Trap: Use the present in the if clause even for the future: "I'll go if it doesn't rain".

  • If you study, you will get good grades.
  • Unless you study, you will not get good grades.
  • The mayor would have lost if the labor unions had not supported him.

Quick checklist

  • Is it a dependent clause starting with a subordinating conjunction?
  • Does it answer when, where, why, how, or under what condition?
  • Comma if it comes first; usually none if it comes last.
  • One connector only: no because…so, no although…but.

Core definition

An adjective clause is a dependent clause that works as an adjective. It modifies a noun or pronoun (the antecedent) and begins with a relative pronoun or relative adverb.

Trap: It gives more information about a noun; it cannot stand alone.

  • The man who lives next door is a doctor.
  • I have a friend who lives in Canada.
  • This is the place where we first met.

1. Relative pronouns and adverbs

TypeWords
Relative pronounswho, whom, whose, that, which
Relative adverbswhen, where (also why)

Trap: Choose by function: whose shows ownership; when, where, why describe time, place, reason.

  • The book that you lent me was interesting.
  • The student whose phone was stolen is crying.
  • I'll never forget the day when I met you.

2. Position and verb agreement

Put the clause right after its antecedent. The verb in the clause agrees in number with the antecedent.

When a clause modifies a whole sentence, it comes at the end, always uses which, and is always nonrestrictive.

Trap: A misplaced clause changes the meaning: "in his friend's car that he had just bought" suggests the car was just bought.

  • He left the gift that he had just bought in his friend's car.
  • An employee who works part-time usually receives no benefits.
  • Employees who work part-time usually receive no benefits.
  • The team won the championship, which shocked the opponents.

3. Restrictive vs nonrestrictive

Trap: Commas change meaning: they mark information the sentence could do without.

  • The book that I borrowed from the library is due tomorrow.
  • Students who study hard usually perform well in exams.
  • My brother, who lives in Cambodia, is visiting next week.

Quick checklist

  • Find the antecedent: is the clause right after it?
  • Does the verb agree with the antecedent?
  • Essential information: no commas. Extra information: commas.
  • Modifying a whole sentence? Use which, at the end.

Noun Clauses

Definitions, rules, common traps, and examples.

Underlined = the signal word; blue bold = the noun clause. (added) = not in your course handouts.

Contents
  1. Core definition
  2. 1. Signal words
  3. 2. Functions
  4. 3. Quick patterns
  5. 4. Common mistakes
  6. Quick checklist

Core definition

A noun clause is a dependent clause that functions as a noun. It cannot stand alone and is part of an independent clause. It can be a subject, an object, or a complement.

Trap: The clause keeps statement word order, not question order.

  • What she said surprised everyone. (subject)
  • I don't know that he is honest. (object)
  • The truth is that we are late. (complement)

1. Signal words

Conjunctions: that, whether, if.
Wh-words: what, where, when, why, how, who, whom, whose.

  • I wonder where she is.
  • That he was late surprised us all.
  • She didn't know what to do.

2. Functions

Subject: what you said is true; whether she agrees doesn't matter; how the accident happened remains unclear.
Object of a verb or preposition.
Subject complement: after a linking verb (be, seem, appear, look).

  • That she won the contest surprised everyone. (subject)
  • She asked whether we were ready. (object of verb)
  • Pay attention to what I'm saying. (object of preposition)
  • The problem is that we don't have enough time. (complement)
  • The problem was who would pay for it. (complement)

3. Quick patterns

FunctionExampleConnectors
SubjectWhat you did was wrong.what, that, how, why
Object (verb)She believes that he is honest.that, if, whether
Object (preposition)I'm thinking about what you said.about + what, of + how
ComplementThe question is who will go.who, which, how

4. Common mistakes

WrongRightWhy
I don't know what is he doing.…what he is doing.No inversion in noun clauses.
She said me that she was tired.She said to me that…Use to + person after say.
I wonder that he is coming.I wonder if/whether he is coming.if/whether for yes/no.
The fact which he is late annoys me.The fact that he is late…that, not which.
I don't know where is she.…where she is.No question order.

Quick checklist

  • Does the clause act as a noun (subject, object, complement)?
  • Statement order inside the clause: no inversion.
  • Yes/no content: if or whether. Not: that.
  • After say with a listener: say to me.

Participial Phrases

Definitions, rules, common traps, and examples.

Underlined = the participial phrase; blue bold = the noun it describes. (added) = not in your course handouts.

Contents
  1. Core definition
  2. 1. Types
  3. 2. Position and punctuation
  4. 3. Choosing the right form
  5. 4. Dangling participles(added)
  6. Quick checklist

Core definition

A participial phrase is a participle plus its modifiers, objects, or complements. It acts as an adjective and describes a noun or pronoun, making the sentence shorter and smoother.

Trap: Place it near the noun it modifies. Compare adjective clauses, which also describe nouns.

  • The girl standing near the door is my cousin.
  • The man sitting near the window is my uncle.

1. Types

TypeFormMeaning
Present-ingongoing or simultaneous action
Past-ed / -encompleted action or passive state
Perfecthaving + past participleaction completed before the main verb
  • Running through the park, the athlete felt energized.
  • Exhausted from the long day, she fell asleep quickly.
  • Having finished her homework, Maria went out to play.

2. Position and punctuation

Beginning: sets the scene; comma after.
Middle: adds detail; commas around it if nonrestrictive.
End: extra information.

Trap: Commas mark non-essential information. Essential phrases take none: "The girl standing near the door…".

  • Smiling brightly, the child opened the gift.
  • The book, written in 1925, is a classic.
  • He left the room, slamming the door behind him.

3. Choosing the right form

Active meaning takes the -ing form; passive meaning takes the past participle.

Trap: Don't use the base verb or simple past: not "the woman wear", "the book wrote", "the house build".

  • The students playing in the playground are excited.
  • The book written by George Orwell is famous.
  • The house built in 2010 is modern.

4. Dangling participles (added)

The phrase must describe the noun right next to it. If that noun can't do the action, the phrase dangles.

Trap: "Running down the street, the car hit a pedestrian" says the car was running.

  • Wrong: Covered in mud, the football game was postponed.
  • Better: Covered in mud, the players left the field. (added)

Quick checklist

  • Participle + modifiers, describing a noun?
  • Present -ing, past -ed/-en, or perfect having + p.p.?
  • Is it next to the noun it describes?
  • Comma if the information is not essential.

Subject-Verb Agreement: Advanced

Definitions, rules, common traps, and examples.

Underlined = the true subject; blue bold = the correct verb. [Brackets] = phrases to ignore. (added) = not in your course handouts.

  4. 3. Along with, together with, including, in addition to, as well as, coupled with
  5. 4. Joined subjects: and, or, nor
  6. 5. Indefinite pronouns, each and every
  7. 6. Confusing and uncountable nouns
  8. 7. Amounts, numbers, percents, and fractions

  10. 9. Inverted subjects (verb before subject)

Core definition

A verb matches its true subject (the head noun): singular with singular, plural with plural. The nearest noun is not always the subject.

Quick method: Cross out prepositional phrases, comma phrases, and relative clauses. Then match the verb to the main noun that is left.

  • The boy [in brown shoes] is Mike.
  • The success [of future negotiation on independence or greater autonomy] is linked to Serbia's desire for EU membership.

1. Finding the true subject

The noun inside a prepositional phrase (of, in, on, for…) is never the subject.

Cross out the extras. What remains is the subject and verb.

Ignore: prepositional phrases, appositives set off by commas or dashes, participial phrases (founded in 1969), and relative clauses (that was blowing…).

Trap: A plural noun right before the verb makes you want a plural verb.

Trap: Long phrases between the subject and verb can hide the real subject.

  • The subject [of these books] is dogs.
  • The work [of the "UN family" on such matters as trade, food, aviation, refugees, drugs and health] has mostly been done through its "specialized agencies".
  • The massive diversion, [for irrigation of cotton-growing areas, of water from the Amu Darya (Oxus) and Syr Darya rivers,] had caused great environmental damage.
  • The idea [of a holy war (jihad) against infidels] is as old as Islam.
  • The Islamic Conference Organization (ICO), [founded in 1969,] had 57 members by 2001.
  • The new practices [evolved in the UN's six decades of activity] have often required the use of soldiers…
  • Predictions [that Britain's entry into the European Community in 1973 would destroy the Commonwealth] proved wrong.

2. Collective nouns

A collective noun names a group (company, government, department, team, club, association, organization). It takes a singular verb as one unit and a plural verb when the members act individually.

Trap: Mixing styles in one text. Pick one and stay consistent. British English often treats sports teams and clubs as plural.

  • The company has approved the budget proposal.
  • The marketing department is doing an impressive job.
  • The government is spending millions of dollars to combat crime.
  • (British) Barcelona play very well.

3. Along with, together with, including, in addition to, as well as, coupled with

These phrases are interrupters, so they do not make the subject plural. The verb agrees with the subject before them.

Trap: "And" adds to the subject; "along with" does not. Also, in "a group of … including X", group is the subject.

  • The Prime Minister, along with his diplomats, attends the 42nd ASEAN Summit in Indonesia.
  • A group of Cambodian diplomats––including the Prime Minister––attends the 42nd ASEAN Summit in Indonesia.
  • The man (in addition to his daughter) lives here.

4. Joined subjects: and, or, nor

And: Two nouns joined by "and" are normally plural. They take a singular verb when they name one thing, dish, or process.

Trap: The slides list fish and chips, peanut butter and jelly, macaroni and cheese, and teaching and learning as one-unit pairs. If you stress two separate items, a plural verb is possible (added).

Or / nor: The verb agrees with the subject closest to it.

Trap: The order of the subjects changes the verb. Put the plural subject last to sound natural.

  • Monitoring and Evaluation needs to be conducted upon completion of the project.
  • Fish and chips is a classic meal. (added)
  • Macaroni and cheese is easy to make. (added)
  • Neither Ben nor his children enjoy the movie.
  • Neither the children nor Ben enjoys the movie. (added)
  • Either the sub-monitors or the monitor has to submit the form to the administration office.

5. Indefinite pronouns, each and every

Singular: each, every, either, neither, nobody, and every- / some- / any- / no- words (everyone, someone, anyone, no one, nothing, anything); also each / neither / any one of + plural noun.
Plural: both, few, many, several.
Depends on the noun: some, all, most, none, any.

Trap: "Everyone" and "each student" feel plural in meaning but are singular in grammar.

Trap: "Each of the members" and "neither of the pictures" look plural, but the subject is each / neither.

  • Each student is going to be given an A+ grade.
  • Each of the committee members finishes reading the instructions.
  • Neither of the pictures was appealing.
  • Nobody in the classroom was able to answer.
  • Few students were absent.

Trap: Criteria, bacteria, and alumni are plural. Data is plural in formal academic writing (singular use is growing). Nationalities like "the Chinese" take a plural verb.

Uncountable nouns (furniture, advice, information, equipment, luggage, news) take a singular verb. Police, people, cattle and clergy take a plural verb.

Trap: "The police was" and "the furniture are" are errors. Use "a piece of advice" to count.

  • Physics is a required course. (added)
  • The news is good. (added)
  • The criteria are clear; the criterion is fairness. (added)
  • The data show a clear trend. (added)
  • The Chinese are attending. (added)
  • All the furniture I bought is at my home now.
  • A great deal of advice was given after the meeting.
  • The police were told to be careful.

7. Amounts, numbers, percents, and fractions

Amounts: A plural amount of money, time, or distance seen as one total takes a singular verb. If you count the individual items, the verb is plural.

Trap: "Four hundred students" is one unit when it describes the turnout, but separate people when they do something.

A number of / the number of: A number of + plural noun takes a plural verb (it means "several"). The number of takes a singular verb because the subject is "number".

Other "of" phrases: a majority of follows the noun after "of"; a group / team / contingent of is one unit. (added: the slide only lists these phrases)

Trap: The two phrases look almost the same but change the verb.

Percent and fractions: With percent and fractions (half, one-third, two-thirds, a fifth, a quarter, three quarters), the verb agrees with the noun after "of". A countable plural takes a plural verb; an uncountable or singular noun takes a singular verb.

The percentage of is singular.

Trap: "Percent" and "half" don't decide the verb. The noun after "of" does.

  • Five hundred dollars is a huge amount of money for a construction worker.
  • Three hours is long enough to complete the journey.
  • Four hundred students is a big turnout.
  • Four hundred students register for the event.
  • A number of refugees from Honduras have fled to the United States through Mexico.
  • The number of refugees from Syria to Eastern Europe keeps on rising.
  • Five percent of the students come from the provinces.
  • Ten percent of the water is contaminated.
  • The percentage of drinkable water is low.
  • The percentage of female students is rising.

8. Noun clauses as subjects

A noun clause (that…, what…, whether…) used as a subject takes a singular verb. Two noun clauses joined by "and" take a plural verb.

Trap: The plural noun inside the clause ("students") is not the subject of the main verb.

  • That a lot of students come from provinces this year is surprising.
  • That he is a good salesperson and that he instills a strong sense of nationalism have earned him so much support among working-class Americans.

9. Inverted subjects (verb before subject)

In inverted sentences, the real subject comes after the verb. "Here" and "there" are never subjects.

A gerund subject (Going on a diet) is singular.

Trap: The word before the verb is not always the subject.

  • Going on a diet requires a lot of determination.
  • Standing just outside the shop are my parents.
  • Now comes the difficult part.
  • Here are your glasses.

10. Relative clauses and 'one of'

A who / that / which clause takes its verb from the noun right before it.

In "one of the + plural noun", the main verb agrees with one. A following who-clause agrees with the plural noun.

Trap: The nearest noun is not always the one the verb agrees with. In "the children of the man who works with me", works agrees with man and the main verb agrees with children.

  • The children of the man who works with me have broken the window.
  • One of my friends has asked a question.
  • She is one of the students who work late.

Final checklist

  • Find the head noun. Ignore prepositional phrases and interrupters.
  • Is the subject one unit (money, time, group, title) or separate individuals?
  • With or/nor, match the nearer subject. With "of", match the noun after it (percent, half) or the head (number, percentage).
  • If the verb comes first, locate the real subject after it.
